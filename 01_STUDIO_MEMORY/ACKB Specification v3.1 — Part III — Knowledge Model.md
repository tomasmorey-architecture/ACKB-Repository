
# Part III — Knowledge Model

The Knowledge Model defines how architectural knowledge is represented, created, validated, transformed and preserved throughout the lifecycle of an Architectural Knowledge Base.

Knowledge is considered the primary asset managed by ACKB.

Documents, conversations, simulations, images and external references are regarded as information sources from which knowledge emerges through interpretation and reasoning.

The purpose of this part is to define a stable conceptual model capable of preserving architectural intelligence independently of software implementations, AI models or organizational changes.

---

# 7. Knowledge Model

Knowledge within ACKB is not considered static.

Architectural understanding evolves continuously as projects progress, new evidence becomes available and architects refine their interpretations.

Consequently, ACKB models knowledge as a living cognitive entity whose value increases through continuous refinement rather than simple accumulation.

Every persistent knowledge element follows a common lifecycle.

---

## 7.1 Knowledge Creation

Knowledge Creation represents the process through which new architectural understanding enters the repository.

Knowledge may originate from multiple sources, including:

- architectural observations;
- site analysis;
- conversations;
- project documentation;
- bibliographic references;
- regulations;
- BIM models;
- GIS datasets;
- simulations;
- design sketches;
- previous projects;
- expert knowledge;
- AI reasoning.

Creation does not necessarily imply validation.

New knowledge may initially exist as tentative observations, hypotheses or interpretations requiring further evaluation.

The objective of the creation phase is to capture architectural thinking before valuable information is lost.

---

## 7.2 Knowledge Validation

Validation establishes the reliability, consistency and applicability of knowledge.

Not every piece of information deserves permanent inclusion within the repository.

Validation may involve:

- human review;
- expert judgement;
- consistency checking;
- comparison with existing knowledge;
- evidence verification;
- semantic integrity assessment;
- conflict detection.

Validated knowledge becomes part of the persistent cognitive memory of the organization.

The validation process should remain transparent and traceable.

---

## 7.3 Knowledge Evolution

Architectural knowledge continuously evolves.

As projects develop, new observations frequently modify previous interpretations rather than replacing them.

ACKB therefore preserves knowledge evolution instead of overwriting historical understanding.

Knowledge evolution may include:

- refinement;
- extension;
- reinterpretation;
- contextual adaptation;
- specialization;
- generalization.

Every evolution preserves explicit relationships with previous versions.

Knowledge should therefore be viewed as an evolving intellectual lineage rather than a collection of disconnected facts.

---

## 7.4 Knowledge Consolidation

Consolidation represents the transformation of project-specific knowledge into reusable organizational knowledge.

Not every lesson learned during a project should remain confined to that project.

When architectural experience demonstrates broader applicability, it may be promoted into higher levels of organizational memory.

Typical examples include:

- design methodologies;
- reusable strategies;
- environmental principles;
- construction knowledge;
- decision patterns;
- best practices.

Consolidation enables continuous organizational learning across multiple projects.

---

## 7.5 Knowledge Archiving

Architectural knowledge rarely becomes obsolete.

Instead, its relevance changes over time.

Rather than deleting knowledge, ACKB promotes controlled archiving.

Archived knowledge remains accessible while clearly distinguished from active knowledge.

Archiving preserves:

- historical reasoning;
- previous project states;
- obsolete methodologies;
- superseded interpretations;
- earlier design alternatives.

This approach guarantees the long-term continuity of architectural memory.

---

# 8. Composite Knowledge

Individual Knowledge Objects rarely provide sufficient understanding for complex architectural reasoning.

Meaning emerges through the structured combination of multiple interconnected knowledge elements.

Composite Knowledge represents these higher-order cognitive structures.

Rather than replacing individual Knowledge Objects, Composite Knowledge organizes them into coherent intellectual systems.

---

## 8.1 Purpose

The objective of Composite Knowledge is to represent architectural understanding at multiple levels of abstraction.

While individual Knowledge Objects capture atomic concepts, Composite Knowledge captures architectural systems.

Examples include:

- Place Models;
- Project Strategies;
- Environmental Models;
- Design Narratives;
- Material Systems;
- Cognitive Maps;
- Interpretative Systems.

Composite Knowledge therefore bridges isolated observations and holistic architectural understanding.

---

## 8.2 Cognitive Composition

Composite Knowledge is not a simple collection of documents.

It represents an organized network of semantically related cognitive objects.

Each component contributes a distinct aspect of understanding while preserving its own identity.

Composition is therefore relational rather than hierarchical.

The same Knowledge Object may participate simultaneously in multiple Composite Knowledge structures.

---

## 8.3 Emergent Knowledge

An essential characteristic of Composite Knowledge is emergence.

The resulting understanding is often richer than the sum of its constituent objects.

For example:

- multiple environmental observations may produce a climatic strategy;
- several historical references may generate an interpretative framework;
- numerous design decisions may reveal an architectural methodology.

ACKB explicitly recognizes emergent knowledge as one of the principal mechanisms through which architectural intelligence develops.

---

## 8.4 Composite Knowledge Evolution

Composite structures evolve continuously.

As new Knowledge Objects are incorporated, relationships are refined and interpretations mature.

Composite Knowledge therefore remains dynamic throughout the project lifecycle.

Rather than preserving fixed models, ACKB maintains continuously evolving cognitive systems.

---

## 8.5 Reusability

Composite Knowledge may become reusable organizational knowledge.

Successful project strategies, conceptual models and interpretative systems may later serve as reference structures for future projects.

The repository therefore supports knowledge transfer not only through individual facts but through complete reasoning systems.

---

# 9. Metadata Schemas

Metadata provides the semantic infrastructure required to manage architectural knowledge throughout its lifecycle.

Within ACKB, metadata is not considered supplementary information.

Metadata constitutes an integral part of every cognitive object.

It enables identification, interpretation, traceability, governance and interoperability across the repository.

Every persistent entity shall possess a standardized metadata structure appropriate to its cognitive role.

---

## 9.1 Knowledge Metadata

Knowledge Metadata uniquely identifies every Knowledge Object and describes its semantic characteristics.

Typical attributes include:

- unique identifier;
- title;
- description;
- knowledge category;
- cognitive domain;
- confidence level;
- author;
- creation date;
- current status;
- associated projects;
- semantic tags.

Knowledge Metadata enables consistent organization and retrieval throughout the repository.

---

## 9.2 Memory Metadata

Memory Metadata describes the persistence and organizational role of stored knowledge.

Typical attributes include:

- memory level;
- persistence policy;
- retention strategy;
- synchronization rules;
- ownership;
- accessibility;
- archival status.

These attributes govern the long-term behavior of organizational memory.

---

## 9.3 Reference Metadata

Reference Metadata describes external knowledge sources.

It includes both bibliographic information and the architectural relevance assigned by the architect.

Typical attributes include:

- bibliographic description;
- publication information;
- source type;
- author;
- publication date;
- licensing information;
- reliability assessment;
- project relevance;
- Reference Intent.

The inclusion of **Reference Intent** constitutes one of the principal innovations introduced in ACKB v3.1.

The repository therefore preserves not only what a reference contains but also why it matters for a specific architectural project.

---

## 9.4 Interpretation Metadata

Interpretation Metadata describes the conceptual meaning assigned to knowledge.

Typical attributes include:

- interpretative framework;
- interpretation scope;
- supporting evidence;
- confidence;
- contextual assumptions;
- related observations;
- affected design domains.

Interpretation Metadata explicitly separates objective observations from subjective architectural understanding.

---

## 9.5 Framework Metadata

Framework Metadata describes reusable conceptual structures.

Examples include:

- Interpretative Frameworks;
- Design Frameworks;
- Evaluation Frameworks;
- Organizational Frameworks.

Typical attributes include:

- framework type;
- conceptual purpose;
- applicability;
- relationships;
- associated knowledge;
- responsible authors;
- version;
- evolution history.

Framework Metadata enables frameworks to become reusable organizational assets.

---

## 9.6 Version Metadata

Architectural knowledge evolves continuously.

Version Metadata preserves the complete historical evolution of every persistent entity.

Typical attributes include:

- version identifier;
- creation date;
- previous version;
- successor version;
- modification summary;
- responsible agent;
- approval status.

Version Metadata guarantees reproducibility and historical continuity.

---

## 9.7 Trace Metadata

Trace Metadata records the provenance and reasoning lineage of cognitive objects.

Typical attributes include:

- originating source;
- contributing agents;
- supporting evidence;
- related reasoning;
- dependent decisions;
- derived knowledge;
- downstream impacts.

Traceability ensures complete transparency throughout the architectural reasoning process.

---

## Metadata Consistency

Although different cognitive objects require different metadata schemas, all schemas shall satisfy a common set of principles.

Every metadata structure should:

- uniquely identify its associated cognitive object;
- preserve semantic consistency across the repository;
- support long-term organizational learning;
- enable interoperability between specialized agents;
- facilitate explainability and traceability;
- remain extensible without compromising backward compatibility.

Metadata is therefore understood not merely as descriptive information but as the semantic infrastructure through which ACKB organizes, connects and preserves architectural intelligence over time.
